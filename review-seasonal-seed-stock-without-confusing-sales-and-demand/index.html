Review seasonal seed stock without confusing sales and demand

At the end of a seed-selling season, sales figures tell you what left the shelf. They do not automatically tell you everything customers wanted. An item may have sold slowly because the description was unclear, while another may have stopped selling because it ran out during the busiest week. A useful stock review combines the numbers with the circumstances that produced them.

Reconstruct the selling window

For each article, record when it became available, when it was displayed prominently and whether it was unavailable during the season. Compare lines over similar selling windows where possible. A late arrival should not be judged by the same total as a line that had a full season of visibility. Keep changes in display, price or description beside the timeline.

Note customer requests that could not be fulfilled. These observations do not become guaranteed orders, but they help explain gaps in the sales record. Distinguish a request for a specific named variety from a general interest in the crop. That difference affects whether the next buying decision concerns one article or the structure of the category.

Separate familiar lines from experiments

Assess a dependable core item against its established role. Did it remain available when customers expected it? Did it support repeat business? For a trial line, ask the question that justified introducing it. A modest sales total may still be useful if the item tested a new customer interest and produced clear information about what those shoppers value.

Avoid expanding every successful experiment immediately. Check whether the result depended on a one-off promotion or a narrow group of enthusiastic buyers. Consider a measured increase with another review point. Equally, do not remove a slow line without checking whether shoppers could find and understand it in the first place.

Review remaining seed deliberately

Identify remaining quantities by article and lot. Check the supplier's handling instructions and the records available for the material. A stock figure alone cannot establish future germination performance. Where seed is being considered for carry-over, resolve the relevant quality and documentation questions through an appropriate process rather than relying on how the container looks.

Keep the commercial decision separate from the biological assessment. Your business may choose to reduce a line because it ties up space or packing time even when the seed remains usable. Conversely, strong previous sales do not remove the need to assess the suitability of material intended for a later season. These are different decisions and deserve different evidence.

Turn findings into next season's actions

Give each retained or changed line a clear action: maintain quantity, adjust the timing, improve the description, trial a smaller amount or stop reordering. Record the reason in one sentence. That note makes the next purchasing discussion more efficient and prevents the team from repeating a debate whose evidence has already been collected.

Invite the person who packed the seed and the person who answered customer questions to the review. They may identify handling or description problems that the sales spreadsheet cannot show. Record those observations as explanations to investigate, rather than automatically treating each impression as a proven cause.

Finally, compare the proposed range with storage and packing capacity. A larger buying list is not an improvement if the team cannot label, present and support it accurately.
