Keep seed lot records useful from receiving to retail packing

Seed traceability is easiest to preserve when it is built into ordinary handling. A delivery arrives with labels, documents and article references; later, the same material may be divided among storage containers and retail packets. Each transfer creates an opportunity to lose the connection between the seed and its source. A practical record follows those transfers without relying on one person's memory.

Receive identity before quantity

At receiving, compare the delivery documents with the purchase record. Check the article designation, supplier reference, quantity unit and any lot information provided. Keep discrepancies visible while they are investigated. A container that looks similar to a previous delivery should not be treated as the same lot merely because the variety name matches.

Assign an internal receiving reference and connect it to the original documents. Photograph labels if that helps your workflow, ensuring the images remain readable. Keep an actual transcription of essential identifiers as well, because a photograph stored without a searchable reference is difficult to retrieve when a question arises months later.

Keep lots physically distinguishable

Store separate lots in clearly identified containers or locations. If a large bag is divided for handling, each resulting container needs a durable link to the original receiving record. Write the identity before moving the contents, rather than planning to add a label afterwards. Temporary unlabelled containers are a predictable source of confusion during interruptions.

Avoid casually combining remaining quantities from separate lots. Mixing may complicate the ability to answer which material went into a retail batch. Where a business has an established procedure for any such operation, its records need to explain the components and subsequent destinations. For many small retailers, maintaining separation is simply easier to manage.

Record the packing event

Give each packing run a reference connected to the source lot, date and retail article. Record the amount issued, the quantity packed and any remainder returned to storage using a consistent unit. An unexplained difference should trigger a check of the measurement and handling record, not an invented adjustment that makes the spreadsheet balance.

Keep the version of the retail label used for the run. Product descriptions and artwork change over time, and the current file may differ from what was printed previously. A dated label copy helps the team answer a customer question about a particular packet without assuming that every edition carried identical wording.

Practise retrieving an answer

Choose a completed retail batch and ask a colleague to trace it back to the receiving record. Then choose a received lot and identify the packing runs associated with it. This simple exercise shows whether the system works in both directions. It also reveals missing identifiers and files that are stored somewhere only one person knows.

Include returns and unused packing material in the procedure. A label left beside the next filling run can create the wrong association even when the seed containers are correctly marked. Clear the work area between articles and check the next identity before restarting.

The record system should suit the size of the business. A small operation can use a disciplined spreadsheet and labelled folders; a larger one may need connected inventory software. The important feature is a stable link between physical material and recorded identity. Our provides a related purchasing perspective. Review the process after each discrepancy and fix the handover that allowed information to disappear, so traceability remains a routine business habit rather than a reconstruction exercise.
